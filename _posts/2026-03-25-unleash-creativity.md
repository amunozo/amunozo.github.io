These days, I've encountered many times the following meme:

()The person appearing in it is Rick Rubin, a semi-mitological music producer known to be involved in lots of the most famous hits of the last decades. Rubin is known for having a minimalistic, non-interventionist style of producing, making the artists feel relaxed and letting them work in the creative aspect. He's also famous for, as displayed on the meme, not having any music or technical ability, but a extremely good taste in what's going to be a hit.

I believe that there is a lot of truth in that meme. A lot of the criticism to autonomous agentic coding tools comes from technical people, arguing that you need to know well what you're doing to make safe and bug free software. And I agree when the case is working on technical, complex software.

However, a lot of software is just boilerplate, creative stuff, in which the technical and coding layers are preventing lots of creative people with lots of taste into developing independently their ideas, at least to create a strong prototype.

Some examples of these cases are the Claude Competition (?) in which most of the winners were not technical people. Ethan Mollitick (?) also noticed that his non-technical MBA students were able to prototype or even finish good products in a couple of days using Claude Code.

Maybe this is a naïve, too optimistic view, but I think that technical people will still be relevant in those places where challenges are technical as these are non-trivial problems in which the details of tech stack, system architecture, etc., are the key of the problem. However, problems where the main innovation or topic is a creative, artistic or business goal can be sometimes be solved with boring, boilerplate code in which these systems excel. Once the rest of society catch up with the use of these tools, people with good taste and the capabilities of articulate their emotions and intentions can take a minimalistic non-interventionist approach, transmitting our feelings and ideas to the coding agents and letting them handle the technical aspects.

Crucially, the tinkering of millions (or billions) of people from different backgrounds will inevitably result in new interesting works that cannot arise only by coders, but for other peoples that face different problems every day. It is a time not to gatekeep these wonderful tools but to encourage its use to the rest of society, so we can all benefit for their wonderful ideas and what can be done by people who has not technical knowledge but is tremendously creative.
